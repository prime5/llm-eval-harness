The key to doing great work is to find something you're genuinely interested in and then work very hard at it. Neither ingredient alone is sufficient. Interest without effort produces nothing. Effort without interest produces competent but not great work.

Great work almost always requires an original contribution. You have to discover something new, build something that didn't exist, or say something that hasn't been said. Imitation can make you competent, but it won't make you great.

The most reliable way to find work you love is to try lots of things. When you're young, you don't yet know what you'll find interesting. The only way to find out is to try, and to pay attention to what makes you want to keep going even when it's hard.

Curiosity is the engine of great work. The best researchers, writers, engineers, and artists are driven by genuine curiosity about their subject. They don't just want to know the answer; they want to understand everything about the problem.

One underappreciated aspect of great work is that it often requires you to work on problems that others have dismissed as too hard or not worth solving. The frontier of knowledge is littered with problems people gave up on. That's often where the most interesting work lies.

Consistency matters more than intensity. Working on something every day, even briefly, compounds. Irregular bursts of effort are usually less effective than steady engagement. The people who produce the most great work are often the most consistent, not the most brilliant.

Taste is prerequisite to great work. You have to be able to recognize what's good before you can produce what's good. The painful early phase of any creative endeavor is when your taste exceeds your ability. Keep going; the ability will catch up.

Working on hard problems makes you better at solving problems. The way to become a great engineer or scientist or writer is to work on things that push the boundary of your current abilities. Easy work makes you comfortable; hard work makes you capable.

Great work is usually collaborative, even when it looks like the work of one person. Behind most individual contributors are communities of practice, mentors, and colleagues who shaped the work. Acknowledging this doesn't diminish the contribution; it explains the conditions that made it possible.

The most important thing is to start. Many people talk about the great work they'll do someday. The people who actually do great work started. They produced bad work first, then mediocre work, and eventually good work. There is no shortcut that skips the early stages.
