The way to get startup ideas is not to try to think of startup ideas. If you do that, you produce ideas that sound plausible but are wrong in a way that's hard to see: you've made up a demand for something, rather than responding to real demand.

The best startup ideas are the ones that seem like bad ideas at first. They're things that seem too niche, or seem like they'd have too small a market. But the founders know something that other people don't. Usually they know from their own experience that something is a real problem.

When you have an idea for a startup, ask yourself: who wants this right now, and why do they want it badly enough to use a half-finished product? If you can't answer that, you don't have a startup idea. You have a project.

The most dangerous wrong answer is a big company. When someone says the target market is enterprises or Fortune 500s, that's often a sign they haven't found the real users who desperately need this.

Counterintuitively, the best way to find startup ideas is to notice problems you experience yourself. If you're living in the future -- meaning you're using technology that doesn't widely exist yet -- you'll notice things that are missing. Those missing things are often startup ideas.

Another approach is to look at what's changed recently. If something is newly possible or newly cheap, look for businesses that are only now worth starting. Infrastructure changes are particularly fertile territory for new startup ideas.

Many successful startups began as what founders called "toys." They seemed too narrow or too lightweight to be real businesses. But those toy-like qualities turned out to be features, not bugs. They let the product be exactly what real users needed.

Startup ideas are not necessarily new discoveries. Often they're old ideas applied to new markets, or new technologies applied to old problems. What matters is whether there's a genuine unmet need, not whether the concept is novel.

A common mistake is optimizing for the idea rather than the market. You can't reason your way to a great startup idea. You have to find the people who are genuinely frustrated with the status quo and build for them. Their pain is your map.

The ideal startup idea is one that a large established company couldn't or wouldn't copy. Either because it's too niche for them to bother, or because it conflicts with their existing business model. Startups win by going where the big companies can't follow.
